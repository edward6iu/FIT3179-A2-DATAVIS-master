Melbourne is flat, compact in its inner suburbs, and mild for much of the year: good conditions for riding a bike. Yet most short trips are still made by car. This page follows four questions: who rides, where the infrastructure is, when people ride, and what makes people feel safe enough to start.

01

The Commuter Footprint

Cycling to work is highly concentrated. A handful of inner councils account for most bike commuters, while outer and middle-ring suburbs barely register.

8.6% of Yarra residents ride to work, the highest of any Melbourne council
14× gap between the highest (Yarra) and lowest (Kingston) council shown
72% of trips under 5 km are made by car, as driver or passenger
3% of those same short trips are made by bike

The darkest councils (Yarra, Melbourne, Merri-bek and Darebin) sit within about 10 km of the CBD. They share dense street grids, short distances to jobs, and the oldest parts of the bike network.

Read the map Hover over a council to see its name and exact percentage. Darker green means a larger share of residents ride to work. The colour bands are fixed (<1%, 1–2%, 2–4%, 4–6%, 6%+) so councils can be compared directly.

The short-trip opportunity

A 5 km ride takes about 20 minutes. Even so, cars carry roughly seven in every ten of these short trips. Shifting just a few of those squares to green is the biggest opportunity for cycling in Melbourne.

The Physical Network & Infrastructure

Not all bike lanes are equal. A painted line on a busy road is a very different experience from a kerb-separated path. The map below separates the two.

Annotation Protected corridors (solid green) converge on the CBD, but they break up within a few kilometres. Riders heading north on Sydney Road share a painted lane with trams and parked cars.
Why it matters Off-road trails and protected lanes carry substantial daily volumes. Mapping protection levels alongside rider counts highlights where gaps in physical separation remain across key radial corridors.

A closer look at Merri-bek

A safer network needs more than bike lanes. These six programs cover bicycle facilities, walking, school travel, road safety, streets and planning. In the adopted plan, Streets for People rises to the second-largest allocation among these programs in 2029–30.

The size behind the rankings

Together, the six programs receive $14.24 million in capital works allocations over five years. Rectangle area shows each program’s share of the total. Planning and design account for $4.40 million, road safety accounts for $4.08 million, and bicycle facilities account for $0.92 million.

Two decades of change

Over 20 years, cars lost share to public transport, walking and cycling. The 2021 Census was taken during lockdown, so its numbers show working from home as much as long-term change.

04

Safety, Demographics & Perception

Many Australians are “interested but concerned”: they would ride if it felt safe. Feeling safe depends heavily on the kind of road, and who rides today shows who feels excluded.

The takeaway Women are often called the “indicator species” of a safe bike network. The corridors with the highest female share (Canning Street and St Kilda Road) are quiet streets or protected lanes. Footscray Road, which runs alongside heavy freight traffic, has the lowest. These differences illustrate how strongly perceived road safety and physical separation align with broader participation in everyday cycling.
